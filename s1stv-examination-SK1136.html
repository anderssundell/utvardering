Learning outcomes On successful completion of the course, the student will be able to: Knowledge and understanding Describe the basic theories about foreign policy at different levels-of-analysis (individual, state, system). Describe the foreign policy process and implementation from different decision-making models. Explain foreign policy change based on current academic research. Critically assess the role of the state in foreign policy and the rise of new non-state actors. Describe research designs and methods used in the course literature on foreign policy analysis. Competence and skills Apply theories of foreign policy to empirical cases. Critically discuss and compare the explanatory power of different theories of foreign policy. Independently produce text in accordance with good academic practice, including proper citation technique and use of references. Summarize and present self-produced academic texts in a well-formulated and clear manner. Plan and work collectively in a group. Communicate clearly and proficiently in English both orally and in writing. Judgement and approach Critically reflect on different foreign policy theories that are presented in the course literature and in lectures. Critically assess the research design and methods that is used on the course literature. Lecture Two Neoclassical realist theory: Bridging realism and liberalism in FPA Teacher: Lisbeth Aggestam This lecture explores the two foundational traditions in Foreign Policy Analysis: realism and liberalism. Realism - historically a dominant force in international political theory - offers a state centric perspective of foreign policy, while liberalism considers a wider range of actors and emphasizes the importance of the domestic sources of foreign policy. Neoclassical realist theory serves as a bridge between these two traditions by integrating both the external constraints of the international system and the domestic factors shaping foreign policy outcomes. Lecture 3 Foreign policy decision-making Teacher : Lisbeth Aggestam In this lecture, we will explore how decisions in foreign policy are made by examining foreign policy-making as a system composed of three stages: input (preferences), process (decision - making), and output (action and implementation). We will look at different theoretical approaches, such as the bureaucratic politics model and the cybernetic model, and consider the concept of ‘bounded rationality,’ which suggests that human decision-makers cannot always meet the ideal of perfectly rational choices. A central question we will address is whether foreign policy-making can be accurately explained through rational choice theory, or whether these alternative models offer a more realistic framework for understanding and explaining how foreign policy decisions are reached. Required readings : Smith et al. (2024), Chapters 8-9. pp. 147-179. Brummer and Oppermann (2024), Chapters 6-8 . Supplementary readings : D. P. Houghton (2024), Decision-making approaches and foreign policy-making . In Karbo and Thies (eds.), The Oxford Handbook of Foreign Policy Analysis. Oxford: Oxford University Press. pp - 342-358. Make-up Assignments for Missed Seminars If you miss a seminar, you need to write and submit a make-up assignment that consist of writing a seminar paper of 1,500 words that demonstrates a critical engagement with the required seminar readings applying theory and concepts to relevant empirical events and cases following the seminar instructions. You upload your make-up assignment in the appropriate folder on Canvas. If you are unable to make an oral group presentation, you will be required to do an individual presentation recorded on Zoom – lasting around 10 minutes - and uploaded in the appropriate folder on Canvas. Grading scale The grading scale for this course is Fail (U), Pass (G), Pass with Distinction (VG). To obtain the Pass grade, the student must receive a Pass on all the three forms of assessment (oral presentation, active seminar participation, and written essay). To obtain Pass with Distinction for the entire course, a student must receive Pass with distinction (VG) on the written essay, providing evidence of an outstanding ability to connect FPA theories and concepts to empirical research findings with a high academic standard in terms of structure and language. Academic Writing The three hallmarks of good academic writing are clarity, precision, and intellectual honesty. Unlike some other forms of writing, academic texts should be free from ambiguity, and they should offer the reader plainly stated arguments, not rhetorical tricks. This does not mean that they have to be boring, of course, but it does mean that, whenever there is a conflict between being linguistically elegant and clear, the latter should take precedence. For example, in academic writing, it might be preferable to consistently use the same terms for the same concepts instead of using synonyms, even though this would be dull in other types of writing (such as fiction). To organize the text, a good rule of thumb is to make one point per paragraph. Good academic writing is cumulative; it proceeds from, communicates with, and seeks to contribute to previous work on the topic of choice. It is essential that the author (or authors) gives due credit to previous work. There should never be any doubt as to whether segments of text, ideas, claims or results are the author’s own or drawn from other sources. To ensure this, academic writing requires a transparent system of citation. Never ‘copy and paste’ text from other sources without citing those sources and enclosing direct quotes in quotation marks. Copying text without indicating it is a quotation constitutes plagiarism. Use references consistently and accurately. These requirements hold for all academic texts, from short student assignments and course papers to master theses and academic books and journal articles. AI policy : The use of generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT) for writing assignments is limited to brainstorming and outlining purposes only. Direct copying of AI-generated text is strictly prohibited, and AI must not be used for drafting or writing any part of the assignment. These assignments are designed to foster your critical thinking, analytical skills, and the ability to express original analysis and ideas. The purpose of this policy is to ensure the work remains your own and that you continuously practice your own skills in academic writing. Please note that the final assignment of the written research paper needs to include an AI-declaration . Plagiarism: Academic writing is based on the principle of clarity and transparency regarding the source of the information and knowledge conveyed in the text. In academic writing, we use others’ ideas, thoughts, arguments, opinions, and conclusions, but we make the origin of these clear by citing and referencing. We rely on, communicate with, build upon, and credit previous research, authors, and sources—but we do NOT copy them! Copying is plagiarism, i.e., cheating. You can read more on academic writing and plagiarism on the Canvas course page. The Department of Political Science uses sophisticated plagiarism detection systems. Teachers are also skilled at detecting texts that are not independently written through critical reading. We are obliged to report suspected cheating to the Disciplinary Board at GU. Cheating can result in a warning or suspension from the program. Read more on plagiarism at GU’s student portal.
