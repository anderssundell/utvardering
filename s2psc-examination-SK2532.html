On successful completion of the course, the student will be able to: Knowledge and understanding • Understand and independently apply scientific theoretical perspectives and methods in order to complete an original research project relevant to Political Science. Competence and skills • Creatively, critically and independently identify, formulate and delimit an original scientific question of relevance to Political Science. • Creatively and independently select an appropriate methodological approach to answer a specified scientific question. 1 • Systematically gather and compile empirical data and/or identify and evaluate existing empirical data, sources or secondary literature. • Produce a written report and orally present and defend it. • Publicly discuss another student’s degree project as well as participate in discussions regarding other students’ degree projects. • Independently produce text in accordance with good academic practice, including proper citation technique and use of references. • Communicate clearly and proficiently in English both orally and in writing. Judgement and approach • Critically evaluate the reliability of conclusions and findings of academic research and policy evaluations. • Identify and critically assess ethical issues related to research on human behaviour 2. Preparations before the Master’s thesis course starts Before the Master’s thesis course begins, and alongside the preceding courses, there will be an introductory lecture and the assignment of supervisors. Students are required to submit an abstract outlining the topic of their thesis. Based on this abstract, the course coordinator will suggest a suitable supervisor for each student. Students may also contact potential supervisors directly to request supervision. However, all agreements must be communicated to the course coordinator. If a student has not established contact with a supervisor by the start of the Master’s thesis course, they must inform the course coordinator immediately. The abstract should be around one page and include: • The research problem • The gap in the literature that your thesis aims to address • The research question(s) • Suggested research design/methodology 3. Supervision Students are supervised by a teacher from the Department of Political Science. The supervisory relationship entails both rights and responsibilities. Students have the right to receive advice and support, but they also have an obligation to use the allocated supervision time - 20 hours in total - as constructively and effectively as possible. To ensure productive meetings, students should submit any text they want feedback on in advance of meetings. Supervisors, in turn, are responsible for preparing for meetings by reading submitted texts and offering constructive comments and guidance. The exact timing and structure of supervision meetings should be agreed upon by the student and the supervisor. However, it is the student’s responsibility to ensure that the supervisor has sufficient time to read and comment on the thesis before it is submitted for examination. 2 4. Examination seminars A. Seminar attendance The schedule for the examination seminars will be published on Canvas a few days after thesis submission. Students are encouraged to attend all seminars. Each student is required to present their own thesis at one seminar and to serve as an opponent at another student’s seminar. Each seminar is 90 minutes long, and typically four seminars are held per day. Students are not able to choose the date or time of their assigned seminar. B. Seminar structure: the role of author and opponent The seminar is divided into three main parts: (1) the author’s presentation, (2) a Q&A and discussion between the author and the opponent, and (3) an oral examination conducted by the examiner. The author presents what the thesis is about, its purpose and most central theoretical contribution, which method and material have been used, what the thesis results are, and how these results have contributed to our knowledge. The presentation should last 10-12 minutes. If the time limit is exceeded, the examiner will interrupt the presentation. During the presentation of their thesis, the author may use one A4 page with bullet-point notes for support, but not a fully written-out presentation. The purpose of the presentation is for the author to demonstrate mastery of the subject and the ability to reflect on their contribution. The A4 page is handed to the examiner during the examination. At the thesis examination, the opponent plays an important role alongside the author. After the author’s presentation, the opponent has 30 minutes to discuss strength and weakness of the study in a constructive manner. The opponent selects which parts are particularly important to focus on during the opposition. Given the limited time, the opponent will need to carefully choose themes and questions. In general, opponents should consider the same criteria for evaluating theses as in Table 1 (see below), including: • Are the aim and research question of the thesis relevant to scientific and policy debates and is this relevance clearly explained? • Is the thesis well connected to previous research and theory? Does the thesis convince you that the research questions address a question not answered in this previous research? • Are the questions of the study reasonable, presented clearly, and do they all help to fulfill the overarching objective of the study? • Are the research design, methods and data/materials appropriate to answer the research questions? Is it clear how the study has been carried out? Are the methodological choices reasonable and well argued for? • Are the analyses and results clear and do they address the research questions posed? Do the conclusions follow from the analysis? What are the possible implications of the results? Does the author discuss these? 3 To take on the role of the opponent in one seminar is mandatory, and the performance is graded as either Pass (G) or Fail (U). If the opponent’s performance gets the grade Fail, they will have to submit written comments on the thesis they are evaluating. After the conversation between opponent and respondent has concluded, the examiner will conduct an oral examination with the author. The examiner will ask various questions to the respondent, who will have the opportunity to answer. The oral examination will last about 30 minutes. The questions may vary depending on the thesis content and how the conversation between respondent and opponent unfolded, but may, for example, concern the literature on which the thesis is based, the research problem, the material and methodological choices, policy implications of the study’s results, or how future studies could build on the work carried out. The purpose of the questioning is to give the author the opportunity to demonstrate that they wrote the thesis independently and that the student can reason about both theoretical and empirical aspects and implications of their own investigation. The oral examination is graded as Pass (G) or Fail (U). After the open parts of the seminar, all participants leave the room except for the opponent and the author. The examiner first communicates the grade for the oral examination to the opponent, who then exits. Next, the author receives their grade. The oral examination grade is given first, and only if it is a Pass will the grade for the thesis be announced. 5. Assessment and grading Progress toward the learning outcomes will be assessed through the Master’s thesis and the student’s performance at the final seminar, where students present and defend their work. In the final seminar the student will also act as opponent on another student’s thesis. In addition to this the student is expected to participate actively in the discussions of other students’ theses. A.
