Abstract: A well-functioning society relies on citizens staying informed about current societal and political events. Recent changes in the news media and political information environment 7 pose a threat to this necessity. In the lecture and seminar, we focus on concerns that are associated with obstacles for knowledge acquisition in such changing environments, such as citizens increasingly avoiding the news and encountering misinformation/disinformation. We discuss, furthermore, fact-checking initiatives and media literacy interventions as potential remedies for these problematic developments. Written exam
written exam about halfway through the course. The exam is based on the literature and the lectures covered to this point. When studying, students are encouraged to systematically create their own summaries of each lecture and of the corresponding literature. Such summaries are sometimes called portfolios and become a concrete result of the course for the students to bring with them. They will also help students in the second part of the course as they provide a basis for the research review. Students are also encouraged to study together, discussing interpretations of the literature. Output-heavy part – writing and presenting yourself After the written exam, students start working on a research review aiming at deepening the understanding of a specific area. The topic of the research review is chosen based on the themes from the first part of the course. Students are encouraged to raise questions during lectures/seminars connected to areas of their specific interest. The task of the students is to write a research review by 1) formulating a broad research problem; 2) developing more specific research questions following from the general research problem, as well as 3) answering these research questions analytically based on existing research. 1.3. Academic integrity and use of artificial intelligence We take academic integrity very seriously. To fulfill the course requirements, you therefore have to conduct a self-teaching module on Canvas called Introduction to Academic Honesty on academic integrity. It takes ca 20 minutes and you can try as many times as you like. But you have to pass it to fulfill the course requirements. In this course, the use of generative AI to create content for examination items is completely prohibited. However, it is allowed to use AI tools to improve the language, e.g., in the research review. Students who do not follow this rule will be reported to the Disciplinary Board for using “unauthorized aids” (see GU's rules for disciplinary matters). Add a note in your research review that clarifies how and for what purposes you have used AI tools. To further help you avoid any suspicions of plagiarism, we list below some of the basic–and hugely important–principles for independent academic writing. Any attempt of fraudulent behavior will be documented. • Academic writing is based on the principle of clarity and transparency regarding the origin of the information and knowledge conveyed by the text. • Academic texts are characterized by independent and self-designed writing. • In combination, the above principles mean that in academic writing we use the ideas, thoughts, arguments, opinions, conclusions of others, but we make clear the origin of these by quoting and referring. We communicate with, build on, and credit previous research, authors, sources, but we do not copy them! Copying is plagiarism, which is the same as cheating. • Some examples of plagiarism: 1) Copying an existing text; 2) moving and deleting a few words from an existing text; 3) translating an existing text from another language; 4) copying from a previous assignment you wrote (self-plagiarism). Please note that none of these examples qualify 3 as legitimate academic writing regardless of whether a reference is present or if the text is presented as one's own description and / or analysis. • There should be no doubt whatsoever in the text about which ideas, thoughts, arguments, opinions and conclusions are yours (absent a reference) and which arguments you obtained from other texts (reference to the author included). There are different reference styles (see more about this at http://www.ub.gu.se/skriva/referensstilar/) but a good rule of thumb, for maximum transparency, is to include the reference in the text, like this: (author surname, year, page), i.e. (Sikkink, 2000, 13). • When an author you read uses particular wording to describe something, and you wish to use this exact wording in your text, this wording should be marked as a quote, with a reference that includes a page reference, such as (author surname, year: page). As stated, always including page numbers in the references in the text – whether cited or not – is a good rule of thumb! • To put it simply, you avoid plagiarism 1) by always writing independently (never ‘copy and paste’), 2) by quoting, and 3) by the careful and frequent use of references. • The Department of Political Science uses the Urkund control system against plagiarism. Our teachers are also very good at detecting texts that are not self-authored through our own critical reading experience. We are obliged to report suspicion of cheating to the Disciplinary Board at GU. Cheating can result in a warning or even a suspension from education. • If you use any artificial intelligence for writing your text, you have to add at the end of your review a declaration explaining exactly how you used it. THEME 1: Public opinion and participation (Ann-Kristin Kölln) 2.2. Lecture 1. How and why do citizens engage in politics Abstract: This theme examines how and why citizens engage in politics by linking theories of political opinion, participation, and voting behaviour. It starts from the insight that political opinions are not fixed but constructed, shaped by information, predispositions, heuristics, and framing. Citizens rely on cognitive shortcuts, such as party cues, social group identities, and media frames, to navigate complex political environments. The theme then turns to political participation, conceptualised broadly to include both conventional and unconventional forms of engagement, and explained through the Civic Voluntarism Model, which highlights the roles of resources, psychological orientations, and mobilization. Finally, the theme explores voting behaviour through sociological, socio-psychological, and rational choice models, emphasising social cleavages, party identification, and evaluations of performance. Throughout, the course critically assesses issues of causality and applies theories to real-world cases, encouraging students to evaluate how citizens think, act, and vote in contemporary democracies.
